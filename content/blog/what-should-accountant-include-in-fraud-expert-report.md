When an employer suspects employee fraud and a dispute may proceed to litigation, financial findings might need to be presented in a clear, structured and evidence-based report. A forensic accountant might be instructed to examine financial records, analyse disputed transactions, assess potential losses and explain the basis of their conclusions.

An employee fraud expert report should do more than list suspicious payments or repeat the findings of an internal investigation. Its purpose, scope and contents depend on the questions the expert has been asked to address, the evidence available and the requirements of the relevant proceedings.

For solicitors and employers, understanding what a forensic accounting report may contain can help clarify the issues that need to be investigated and the information that may be required during the instruction process.

## What Is an Employee Fraud Expert Report?

An employee fraud expert report is a report prepared by a suitably qualified expert to address financial or accounting issues arising from suspected or alleged employee fraud.

Depending on the circumstances, the work may involve examining suspected payroll fraud, expense irregularities, unauthorised payments, supplier transactions, diversion of company funds or discrepancies in financial records.

The report may address questions such as:

- Which transactions have been identified for examination?
- What do the available financial records show?
- Are there discrepancies between recorded transactions and supporting documentation?
- Can particular payments or transfers be traced through the available records?
- What financial loss, if any, can be identified or quantified within the scope of the instruction?
- What assumptions, limitations or unresolved issues affect the analysis?

The expert's role is to provide an independent financial analysis within their area of expertise. The report should not assume that suspected misconduct has been established or attempt to determine legal liability.

## 1. The Expert's Qualifications and Relevant Experience

A forensic accountant's report will generally identify the expert's qualifications, professional background and relevant experience.

This information helps the reader understand the basis on which the expert has been instructed to address the financial issues in dispute.

Relevant experience may include work involving:

- Forensic accounting investigations
- Analysis of disputed financial transactions
- Employee fraud allegations
- Financial loss assessment
- Asset tracing
- Accounting record reconstruction
- Expert evidence in civil disputes

The relevant expertise will depend on the issues involved. For example, a matter involving complex payment flows may require different experience from one primarily concerned with payroll records or expense claims.

The report should accurately describe the expert's qualifications and experience without overstating their relevance to the specific issues being considered.

## 2. The Scope of the Instruction

A report should make clear what the expert was asked to examine.

Employee fraud disputes can involve several distinct questions, and a broad instruction to investigate suspected fraud may not adequately identify the financial issues requiring expert analysis.

The scope may cover matters such as:

- Reviewing specified transactions over a defined period
- Examining payments to particular employees or suppliers
- Reconstructing relevant accounting records
- Analysing discrepancies between invoices and payments
- Assessing the financial effect of identified transactions
- Reviewing repayments or other recoveries
- Addressing specific accounting questions raised by the parties

The scope should distinguish the financial questions requiring expert consideration from wider issues that may fall outside the expert's remit.

For solicitors, defining the questions clearly at the outset can help establish which documents are required and what work the expert is expected to undertake.

## 3. Relevant Documents and Financial Evidence

A forensic accounting report should identify the material evidence on which the analysis is based.

The records required will depend on the allegation and the questions being investigated. They may include:

### Banking and Payment Records

Bank statements, payment confirmations and transfer records can help establish the movement of funds, the amounts involved and the identity of recorded recipients.

### Accounting Records

General ledgers, journal entries, reconciliations and accounting system data may help explain how transactions were recorded and identify discrepancies requiring further examination.

### Payroll and Expense Documentation

Payroll reports, expense claims, reimbursement records and supporting receipts may be relevant where the suspected conduct concerns employee payments or expenses.

### Supplier and Procurement Records

Purchase orders, supplier invoices, contracts and payment approvals may help establish whether a payment corresponds to a recorded purchase or service.

### Internal Business Records

Emails, approval records and other relevant communications may provide context for transactions and help explain how particular payments were authorised or processed.

The report should explain the relevance of the material relied upon. Where important records are missing or incomplete, that limitation may need to be identified rather than resolved through unsupported assumptions.

## 4. The Methodology Used to Examine Transactions

A forensic accountant's report should explain the approach used to analyse the financial information.

The appropriate methodology will depend on the nature and complexity of the matter. It may involve reconciling bank statements to accounting records, comparing expense claims with supporting documents, reviewing payment approval trails or reconstructing transactions across a defined period.

For example, where an employer suspects that payments were made to an undisclosed supplier, the analysis may involve examining the supplier records, payment history, relevant accounting entries and available supporting documentation.

The report should explain how the evidence was assessed and how the findings relate to the questions in the instruction.

Where a calculation depends on assumptions, estimates or incomplete records, these should be identified where material. The reader should be able to understand the basis of the analysis and the extent to which the evidence supports the conclusions.

## 5. Findings and Analysis of Disputed Transactions

A report may need to distinguish between transactions that are adequately explained by the available records and those that remain unexplained or require further investigation.

A transaction should not be described as fraudulent solely because it appears unusual or lacks an immediately identifiable explanation.

The analysis may instead identify:

- Transactions that appear inconsistent with the available supporting records
- Payments for which approval documentation has not been located
- Differences between invoiced amounts and recorded payments
- Transfers that require further tracing
- Entries that cannot be reconciled using the information provided

The expert should explain the evidence relevant to each material finding and distinguish recorded facts from interpretations or opinions.

This approach is particularly important where the parties dispute the purpose, authorisation or financial effect of a transaction.

## 6. Quantification of Potential Financial Loss

Where loss assessment falls within the instruction, the report may include an analysis of the financial effect of the transactions under examination.

The calculation should explain which amounts have been included, the records supporting those amounts and any material adjustments or qualifications.

Depending on the circumstances, the analysis may need to consider:

- The amount of each relevant payment
- Whether the business received goods, services or another benefit
- Whether a transaction included a legitimate business expense
- Whether amounts have been repaid or otherwise recovered
- Whether any items remain uncertain or unsupported
- Whether the available records permit a reliable calculation

The total value of suspicious transactions should not automatically be presented as the amount of financial loss. Each item requires consideration in light of the evidence and the purpose of the calculation.

Where the available information does not support a definite figure, the report should explain the limitations and qualify its conclusions accordingly.

## 7. Assumptions, Limitations and Unresolved Questions

A forensic accounting report should make material assumptions and limitations clear.

These may arise where records are missing, transaction descriptions are incomplete, information from different systems does not reconcile or the expert has not been provided with documents needed to address a particular question.

For example, an expert may identify a payment to a supplier but lack the contract, invoice or delivery records needed to assess the commercial context fully.

The report should distinguish what can be established from the documents reviewed from what remains uncertain.

Where the expert cannot reach a definite opinion, that limitation should be explained. This helps prevent readers from treating an incomplete analysis as a conclusive finding.

## 8. Conclusions That Address the Questions in the Instruction

The conclusions should summarise the principal findings and answer the relevant questions within the scope of the expert's work.

Depending on the assignment, this may include identifying discrepancies, explaining transaction patterns, reporting the results of a reconciliation or setting out a financial calculation.

The conclusions should be consistent with the analysis presented in the report. They should not extend beyond the available evidence or the expert's area of expertise.

Where more than one interpretation is possible, the report may need to explain the alternative views and the reasons for the expert's opinion.

The expert should not present an allegation of employee fraud as an established fact merely because a financial discrepancy has been identified.

## 9. Independence and Applicable Court Requirements

Where a report is prepared for civil proceedings in England and Wales, the applicable rules and directions governing expert evidence should be considered.

Under Part 35 of the Civil Procedure Rules and Practice Direction 35, where applicable, an expert's overriding duty is to help the court on matters within their expertise. That duty takes precedence over obligations to the party instructing or paying the expert.

The relevant requirements address matters including the expert's qualifications, material instructions, the basis of opinions, conclusions and the expert's duty to the court. Reports must also contain the required declarations and statement of truth where the rules apply.

The precise requirements depend on the proceedings, the court's directions and the nature of the instruction. Solicitors should confirm the applicable requirements for the particular case rather than assume that one report format will suit every dispute.

An expert report should provide an objective analysis of the relevant financial issues, including material facts that may not support the position of the party who instructed the expert.

## 10. Making the Report Clear and Traceable

A report may contain technically sound analysis but still be difficult to assess if its reasoning is not clearly explained.

The presentation should allow the reader to follow the connection between the source records, the methodology, the findings and the conclusions.

Depending on the assignment, supporting material may include transaction schedules, reconciliations, calculations and references to relevant records.

The report should distinguish clearly between financial information taken directly from the records, calculations derived from that information and the expert's professional opinions.

This structure can help solicitors, the opposing party and the court understand the basis of the financial analysis and identify any matters that remain disputed.

## What Should Solicitors Consider Before Instructing a Forensic Accountant?

Before instructing an expert, solicitors may wish to establish the financial issues that require examination and the purpose for which the report is needed.

Useful considerations include:

- The nature of the suspected or alleged employee fraud
- The specific accounting or financial questions requiring expert analysis
- The relevant period and transactions
- The financial records currently available
- Any missing documents or known evidential limitations
- Whether loss quantification or asset tracing is required
- The applicable procedural requirements and timetable
- The expected scope of work and associated costs

A clear instruction can help the expert understand the issues in dispute and identify what additional information may be needed.

The scope may need to be refined as the financial records are reviewed. Any change should be managed in accordance with the instruction and applicable procedural requirements.

## Conclusion

A forensic accountant's employee fraud expert report may include the expert's qualifications, scope of instruction, relevant financial evidence, methodology, transaction analysis, loss calculations, material assumptions and conclusions.

The precise contents depend on the questions being addressed, the evidence available and the requirements of the relevant proceedings. A report should distinguish suspected misconduct from established financial facts and explain any limitations that affect its findings.

For solicitors and employers, understanding these elements can help clarify what financial issues need to be investigated and what information may be required when instructing an expert.
